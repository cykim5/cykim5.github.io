Custom Motion Hardware for a Liquid Handling Robot

Hardware control, Opentrons, Python, lab automation

Loading the model

Built for an Opentrons liquid handling robot. The stage tilts plates along two axes partway through a run, so liquid pools to one side of each well. Because it is driven from protocol code, tilting becomes an ordinary step in an automated workflow.

The current design is V5, a two motor synchronized tray mechanism. One motor drives a shared pitch frame through a 4:1 gear reduction. A second motor rides on that frame and meshes with both tray gears, so the trays roll together rather than independently. Target range is 15 degrees either side of level on each axis. Two AS5600 magnetic encoders read the angle back, one on pitch and one on a tray's roll.

Designed and built with Raspberry Pi motor control for CMU lab automation coursework, covering the whole device integration path: mechanical design that fits the robot deck, motor control, and the software interface a protocol drives it through.

Gene-ius

LLM agents, knowledge graphs, Python, team of 5

An AI agent that helps laboratories interpret genetic variants of uncertain significance, which account for roughly a third of variants in ClinVar. Across 23 retina and heart genes it ranks which variants to test first and explains whether a variant is harmful, how it breaks the protein, and which disease follows.

Evaluated with a time machine design: the model sees only data available in January 2023 and is graded on variants experts resolved afterwards, so no variant is ever evidence for itself. It found 38 later proven disease variants in its top 500, against 18 for AlphaMissense.

A production data platform I built from scratch at RLWRLD, a robotics company in Seoul, turning robot training footage into structured, model ready data. Cancer Screening Cost-Effectiveness Model

Python, Monte Carlo simulation, SEER data, health economics, team project

A simulation asking whether annual multi-cancer early detection screening is worth its cost, and for whom. It follows simulated people month by month and compares usual care against usual care plus annual screening, run separately for a lower risk and a higher risk group, because a test can be a poor buy on average and a good buy for people at high risk. I built the lung cancer model.

Incidence, stage at diagnosis and survival come from SEER data, with test sensitivity by stage, treatment costs and quality of life weights on top. The headline number is the extra cost per extra year of healthy life gained, reported by risk group and checked against the published results of Raoof et al. (2025).

OncoTarget Scout

Python, public multi-omics data, molecular docking, Phases 1 to 6 complete

A target prioritization pipeline for one group of lung adenocarcinoma patients: tumors carrying STK11 or KEAP1 mutations. These tumors respond poorly to both immunotherapy and chemotherapy, and have no approved therapy aimed at the alteration itself. The pipeline produces a ranked shortlist of candidate genes.

A gene has to earn its rank across independent layers of evidence, so that nothing ranks on one signal alone: activity in tumors against healthy lung in TCGA, functional dependency in DepMap CRISPR screens, expression in the malignant cells themselves rather than the immune cells around them, a safety window against vital normal tissue in GTEx, and the link to patient survival. Ranking the full TCGA cohort of 528 tumors and 59 healthy samples, then splitting it into 143 mutant and 379 wild type tumors, gave a dependency-supported shortlist of 20 genes, led by INTS6 and NFE2L2, the gene that encodes NRF2. The pipeline rediscovered NRF2 on its own, which is the pathway KEAP1 loss activates. A time-split check rebuilt the ranking on DepMap data frozen before 2021, and 12 of the 20 survived. Each gene carries a plain-language reason card, and the top five have a validation plan naming the cell lines and assays that would confirm or kill them.

One scoring bug had been there since Phase 1. A gene's score was the weighted mean over the layers it actually had, renormalized each time, so the fewer layers a gene had the higher its ceiling. Every one of the top 20 was missing the dependency layer and most were non-coding RNAs. The ranking now compares genes only when they carry the layer that holds the most weight. KRAS moved from 4,104th of 28,328 to 157th of 16,711 and EGFR from 5,182nd to 588th, so the pipeline recovers known lung cancer biology without being told to. The shortlist did not change, because it already required dependency support. The headline was wrong while the decision was right.

Phase 4 took UXS1, the third-ranked gene, as far as computation can take it: an enzyme with two solved crystal structures, no known inhibitor, and independent 2025 evidence that it is a selective dependency in KEAP1 mutant lung cancer. I docked 2,757 approved drugs into its active site with AutoDock Vina, then tested whether the ranking could be trusted at all. Docked in the same box, the enzyme's own substrate scored below two thirds of random approved drugs, so the output was not a hit list. The decisive repair came four tries later: every earlier run had used a 2.5 angstrom structure solved with only the cofactor bound, where an arginine folds across the substrate site and leaves no room for the thing that belongs there. Redocking against a 1.21 angstrom structure of the same enzyme, solved with UDP actually in the site, moved the substrate to the 88th percentile and the best natural ligand to the 99th.

Rescreening the library against the open structure, the score inside the control's size range no longer tracks molecular size, and 4.7 percent of the library now beats the substrate where two thirds of it did before. Of the 59 best scoring structures, 5 carry a negative group and touch all four arginines that grip the substrate's phosphate. Vina scores hydrophobic contact and hydrogen bonds and has no term for charge at all, so recovering the site's own chemistry from a library never designed for it is the result. None of the five is a lead. Two are prodrugs whose anionic group exists to be cleaved off, one is built not to cross membranes and UXS1 sits in the Golgi, one is a dye that binds most things. The approved drug library holds no credible binder for this site, which is unsurprising because oral drugs are rarely polyanions. The finding is that the protocol can now tell the difference, and the earlier version demonstrably could not. The next step is a CRISPR experiment that needs no compound.

The work also caught its own errors. Phase 1's top hit turned out to be an artifact of dividing by a near-zero baseline, and Phase 2 fixed it with shrinkage. Phase 4's one promising lead, three related antidiabetic drugs that had ranked together, did not survive the corrected box. KRAS, EGFR and CD274 rank in the middle of the subgroup ranking, which is the correct result: they are not subgroup-selective targets.

Biological Workflow Map

Python, YAML, interactive map

A map of what happens at a biology bench, sorted so that every workflow has exactly one place. Eight domains, 264 workflows, four levels deep. It is the base layer for a larger question: what in a given lab is worth automating, and what the return would be. A lab describes its work in its own words, and those words have to resolve to a shared set of workflows before anything can be scored or compared across labs.

Biology already has plenty of ways to group lab work and none of them is mutually exclusive, because each mixes several axes at the same level. One ELISA is immunology, cancer biology and bioprocess at once. The rule here is that every workflow is filed under the kind of thing it exists to produce, because output is the one attribute that does not overlap. A workflow yields purified RNA, or an engineered cell line, or a number, but never two at once. Field, technique and instrument all overlap, so they became filters over the map instead of branches in it.

The hard cases are the test. PCR run to make DNA for a later step is construction, and qPCR run to produce a number is measurement. A gel run to recover a band is cleanup, and a gel run to report a size is analysis. Centrifuging, mixing and incubating are not in the map at all, because a step that happens inside everything is not a workflow. Automation feasibility is not a branch either but an attribute measured against the map, so a workflow that is manual today and automated in three years does not move, only its score changes.

No existing taxonomy covers wet-lab work as a whole, so there is nothing to validate this against directly. The test that would settle it is assigning several hundred published protocols to leaves and measuring how many land in exactly one, with two people assigning independently to see whether the rule is teachable.

Automation Assessment

JavaScript, no build step

A first screen for deciding which tasks to automate with robots, meant to be used before talking to any vendor. Most robot deployments do not fail because the robot cannot do the work. They fail because someone picked the wrong task, one that looked easy and was not, or one that worked and never paid back.

Every task gets two scores. Feasibility averages three ratings: how complex the motion is, how predictable the object is, and how controlled the workspace is. Business fit is 60 percent financial return, which comes from payback time, and 40 percent strategic value, which covers what money does not, like a role nobody can hire for or a safety risk. Tasks then land in one of four groups: quick wins, strategic bets, easy but low value, and deprioritize.

It is written around a small manufacturer's task list, though the three feasibility axes are not specific to manufacturing. Payback assumes the robot covers all the listed hours, when real deployments ramp up slowly, and the example tasks and costs are illustrative rather than client data.

Lab Automation Payback

JavaScript, no build step

A calculator for when automating one lab workflow step pays for itself. Lab automation is usually pitched on throughput, but the decision gets made on cost, and whether a liquid handler or an automated sample store pays back depends mostly on volume, on how much hands-on time it removes, and on the running costs people leave out of the pitch.

Net savings per year is labor time saved plus fewer reruns, minus the service contract and the extra consumables. Payback is upfront cost divided by monthly net savings. It also reports staff time freed, in hours and in full-time equivalents, and the break-even volume, meaning the samples per day needed to pay back within three years, plus a five-year cash chart.

What it does not capture is part of the tool too: traceability, consistency between operators, and room to grow without hiring. Those are often the real reasons to automate, and when payback is borderline they usually decide it. The presets use illustrative numbers, not data from any company.
