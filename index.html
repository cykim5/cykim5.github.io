Protocol Agent for Instrument Workflows

LLM agents, Python, instrument protocols, Isayev Lab, CMU

An LLM agent that turns a plain language description of an experiment into a structured, executable instrument protocol, so it becomes something a machine can run rather than a paragraph in a notebook.

In progress at the Isayev Lab at CMU, built for the CLARIOstar plate reader. The hard part is generating a protocol the instrument will actually accept, since its documented control commands can only run protocols that already exist.

Two-Axis Tilt Stage for Opentrons

Hardware control, Opentrons, Python, lab automation

Loading the model

Drag to rotate, scroll to zoom. The assembled view is the device as built, exported from the CAD model. The separate parts are shown in the orientation they print in.

Custom motion hardware for an Opentrons liquid handling robot. The stage tilts a plate along two axes partway through a run, so liquid pools to one side of each well. Because it is driven from protocol code, tilting becomes an ordinary step in an automated workflow, called the same way pipetting is.

Designed and built with Raspberry Pi motor control for CMU lab automation coursework, covering the whole device integration path: mechanical design that fits the robot deck, motor control, and the software interface a protocol drives it through. Two stepper driven cams under a platform on a fixed ball give tilt in any direction up to 17 degrees, checked across 25 motor positions in simulation before anything was printed.

Gene-ius

LLM agents, knowledge graphs, Python, team of 5

An AI agent that helps laboratories interpret genetic variants of uncertain significance, which account for roughly a third of variants in ClinVar. Across 23 retina and heart genes it ranks which variants to test first and explains whether a variant is harmful, how it breaks the protein, and which disease follows. The ranking hands a lab a short, evidence backed list to take into physical testing.

Evaluated with a time machine design: the model sees only data available in January 2023 and is graded on variants experts resolved afterwards, so no variant is ever evidence for itself. It found 38 later proven disease variants in its top 500, against 18 for AlphaMissense.

Production Data Platform

Python, PostgreSQL, AWS, AI agents, RLWRLD

A production data platform I built from scratch at RLWRLD, a robotics company in Seoul, turning robot training footage into structured, model ready data. It replaced annotation the team had been doing by hand in spreadsheets, and its pipelines carry each recording through annotation, quality control and analytics.

It cut three hours of manual work per hour of footage to under thirty minutes of review, and a team of ten was using it daily by the end of my internship. It became the data layer the robot foundation model training depended on.

Cancer Screening Cost-Effectiveness Model

Python, Monte Carlo simulation, SEER data, health economics, team project

A simulation asking whether annual multi-cancer early detection screening is worth its cost, and for whom. It follows simulated people month by month and compares usual care against usual care plus annual screening, run separately for a lower risk and a higher risk group, because a test can be a poor buy on average and a good buy for people at high risk. I built the lung cancer model.

Incidence, stage at diagnosis and survival come from SEER data, with test sensitivity by stage, treatment costs and quality of life weights on top. The headline number is the extra cost per extra year of healthy life gained, reported by risk group and checked against the published results of Raoof et al. (2025).

OncoTarget Scout

Python, public multi-omics data, molecular docking, Phases 1 to 4 complete

A target prioritization pipeline for one group of lung adenocarcinoma patients: tumors carrying STK11 or KEAP1 mutations. These tumors respond poorly to immunotherapy and to chemotherapy, carry worse outcomes across treatments, and have no approved therapy aimed at the alteration itself. The pipeline produces a ranked shortlist of candidate genes.

Phase 4 took UXS1, the third-ranked gene, as far as computation can take it. A 2025 Cancer Research paper had independently found UXS1 to be a selective dependency in KEAP1 mutant lung cancer, and it is an enzyme with two solved crystal structures and no known inhibitor, which made it the most actionable target on the shortlist. I docked 2,757 approved drugs into its active site with AutoDock Vina in under seven hours, then tested whether the ranking could be trusted at all. Docked in the same box, the enzyme's own substrate scored below two thirds of random approved drugs. A method that ranks the right answer in the bottom third cannot pick candidates, so the output is not a hit list.

Three repairs were tried against that control. The search box was centred 8.6 angstroms off the crystallographic ligand, a second scoring function did worse than the first, and the natural ligands had been prepared uncharged when the substrate carries about three negative charges at physiological pH. Fixing all three moved the substrate from the 32nd to the 79th percentile, still short of the top few percent that was fixed as the bar before the screen ran. So the target stands and the chemistry route closes. This active site is the kind empirical scoring functions are known to handle worst, and the published biology is that losing UXS1 is what sensitizes these tumors, which points at a degrader or a knockdown rather than an inhibitor. The next step is a CRISPR experiment that needs no compound.

Phase 3 added a single-cell layer from CELLxGENE, 1,953 lung adenocarcinoma cells, and confirmed that 19 of the 20 shortlist genes are active in the malignant cells themselves. A time-split check rebuilt the ranking on DepMap data frozen before 2021: 12 of 20 genes survived the older data, and SPRR1B did not. Each gene now has a plain-language reason card, and the top five have a validation plan naming the cell lines and assays that would confirm or kill them.

A gene has to earn its rank across independent layers of evidence, so that nothing ranks on one signal alone: activity in tumors against healthy lung in TCGA, functional dependency in DepMap CRISPR screens, expression in the malignant cells themselves rather than the immune cells around them, a safety window against vital normal tissue in GTEx, and the link to patient survival. All five layers are in the ranking now. Three checks are fixed before the ranking runs, including a time split that asks whether the method would have surfaced the KRAS G12C inhibitors before they were approved.

Phase 1 tested the method on the full lung adenocarcinoma cohort from TCGA, 528 tumors and 59 healthy samples, ranking 35,273 genes on expression and survival. Phase 2 asked the subgroup question, splitting the cohort into 143 mutant tumors and 379 wild type, and adding CRISPR dependency data from 40 mutant lung cell lines and a safety check from healthy organ data. The dependency-supported shortlist has 20 genes, led by INTS6 and NFE2L2, the gene that encodes NRF2. The pipeline rediscovered NRF2 on its own, which is the pathway KEAP1 loss activates. Thirteen of the 20 hold up under different weightings.

The work also caught its own errors. Phase 1's top hit turned out to be an artifact of dividing by a near-zero baseline, and Phase 2 fixed it with shrinkage. Phase 4's one promising lead, three related antidiabetic drugs that had ranked together, did not survive the corrected box. KRAS, EGFR, and CD274 rank in the middle of the subgroup ranking, which is the correct result. The data shows they are not subgroup-selective targets.

Biological Workflow Map

Python, YAML, interactive map, public

A map of what happens at a biology bench, sorted so that every workflow has exactly one place. Eight domains, 264 workflows, four levels deep. It is the base layer for a larger question, which is what in a given lab is worth automating and what the return would be. A lab describes its work in its own words, and those words have to resolve to a shared set of workflows before anything can be scored or compared across labs.

Biology already has plenty of ways to group lab work and none of them is mutually exclusive, because each mixes several axes at the same level. One ELISA is immunology, cancer biology and bioprocess at once. The rule here is that every workflow is filed under the kind of thing it exists to produce, because output is the one attribute that does not overlap. A workflow yields purified RNA, or an engineered cell line, or a number, but never two at once. Field, technique and instrument all overlap, so they became filters over the map instead of branches in it.

The hard cases are the test. PCR run to make DNA for a later step is construction, and qPCR run to produce a number is measurement. A gel run to recover a band is cleanup, and a gel run to report a size is analysis. Centrifuging, mixing and incubating are not in the map at all, because a step that happens inside everything is not a workflow. Automation feasibility is deliberately not a branch either. It is an attribute measured against the map, so a workflow that is manual today and automated in three years does not move, only its score changes.

This is a synthesis rather than a published standard, and no existing taxonomy covers wet-lab work as a whole, so there is nothing to validate it against directly. The weaknesses are written down with it: the measurement domain is the widest, two domains share a soft seam where staining a slide and imaging it feel like one activity, depth is uneven, and the tie-breaks are reasoned rather than cited. The test that would settle it is assigning several hundred published protocols to leaves and measuring how many land in exactly one, how many fit two or more, and how many fit none, with two people assigning independently to see whether the rule is teachable.

Automation Assessment

JavaScript, no build step, public

A first screen for deciding which tasks to automate with robots, meant to be used before talking to any vendor. Most robot deployments do not fail because the robot cannot do the work. They fail because someone picked the wrong task, one that looked easy and was not, or one that worked and never paid back.

Every task gets two scores. Feasibility averages three ratings: how complex the motion is, how predictable the object is, and how controlled the workspace is. Business fit is 60 percent financial return, which comes from payback time, and 40 percent strategic value, which covers what money does not, like a role nobody can hire for or a safety risk. Tasks then land in one of four groups: quick wins, strategic bets, easy but low value, and deprioritize.

It is written around a small manufacturer's task list, though the three feasibility axes are not specific to manufacturing. The limits are stated in the tool rather than left for the reader to find. Payback assumes the robot covers all the listed hours, when real deployments ramp up slowly, and the ratings are judgment calls. It is a shortlist before an on-site assessment, not a final answer, and the example tasks and costs are illustrative rather than client data.

Lab Automation Payback

JavaScript, no build step, public

A calculator for when automating one lab workflow step pays for itself. Lab automation is usually pitched on throughput, but the decision gets made on cost, and whether a liquid handler or an automated sample store pays back depends mostly on volume, on how much hands-on time it actually removes, and on the running costs people leave out of the pitch.

Net savings per year is labor time saved plus fewer reruns, minus the service contract and the extra consumables. Payback is upfront cost divided by monthly net savings. It also reports staff time freed, in hours and in full-time equivalents, and the break-even volume, meaning the samples per day needed to pay back within three years, with a five-year cash chart and a chart of how payback moves as volume changes.

What it does not capture is part of the tool too: traceability, audit readiness, consistency between operators, and room to grow without hiring. Those are often the real reasons to automate, and when payback is borderline they usually decide it. It also ignores validation downtime and the early learning curve. The presets use illustrative numbers, not data from any company.
